How does the signing process work today? A user needs to sign a PDF, downloads it, searches for an external tool on the internet, signs it, saves it, opens their email client, attaches it and sends it to the next person. This cycle repeats for every signer. [PAUSE] This brings four clear problems: enormous administrative time lost on repetitive tasks; human errors such as emails that get lost or files that never get attached; a lack of traceability — we have no centralized record of who signed or when; and inefficient storage, because the same file gets replicated across dozens of computers.

Electronic Signature or Digital Signature?

Meets
✓ Meets all the technical and legal conditions of Law 25.506 to be a Digital Signature
✗ Except: the certificate was not issued by a Certification Authority licensed by the State
Electronic Signature Valid in FACET's internal administrative scope
Before moving on to the demonstration, I want to clarify the legal framework of the system. Documenso does everything the law requires technically: it applies a mathematical procedure that uniquely identifies the signer, and guarantees the integrity of the document — any later alteration invalidates the signature. But Law 25.506 has an additional requirement to be considered a Digital Signature: Article 9 requires the certificate to be issued by a Certification Authority licensed by the State. We use a self-signed certificate, generated inside the institution — without that external backing. That means the system produces Electronic Signatures, not Digital Signatures. But that does not invalidate it: it has full administrative validity within FACET, for all its internal circuits.

Section 02

Moving to the second section — work objectives. [PAUSE]

Design, implement and deploy a “turnkey” solution for managing and electronically signing PDF documents at the faculty

The proposal replaces that seven-step manual process with an automated four-step one. [PAUSE] Step one: single upload. The lecturer or student uploads the PDF to the system once. No downloads or email forwarding. [PAUSE] Step two: visual configuration. Directly in the web interface, they define who must sign and where each signature goes on the document. [PAUSE] Step three: Documenso's automation engine takes care of the rest — it notifies each signer in turn and manages the flow without manual intervention. [PAUSE] Step four: traceable output. The final document is signed, with a cryptographic seal, publicly verifiable, and stored in FACET's infrastructure.

Before choosing the platform, I surveyed the three most widely used SaaS solutions on the market. Docusign, Dropbox Sign and Adobe Sign have similar prices — between 24 and 25 dollars per user per month. For 20 users that means between 480 and 500 dollars per month, almost 6,000 dollars a year. This justifies looking for an open-source alternative deployable within the institution itself. [PAUSE]

After ruling out the SaaS options, I surveyed the most prominent open-source alternatives on the market. Documenso and DocuSeal are the two projects with the highest adoption — both with more than 12 thousand stars on GitHub, self-hosted, and with a REST API. The main difference lies in the stack: Documenso uses TypeScript with Next.js and PostgreSQL, while DocuSeal uses Ruby on Rails with Vue.js and supports SQLite in addition to PostgreSQL. Both meet the data sovereignty requirements by being deployable on your own infrastructure. [PAUSE]

Documenso's organizations and teams model is an almost exact mapping of FACET's structure. The faculty is the root organization. Each department — DEEC, Physics, Mathematics — becomes a team with its own isolated document space. A lecturer who teaches in two departments simply belongs to both teams, and the platform automatically manages which documents they can see in each context. This eliminates custom configuration and makes adoption immediate. [PAUSE]

Why Documenso?

MinIO

Brevo

SMTP relay for notifications. Free tier: up to 300 emails/day.

OAuth 2.0 with Google Workspace UNT: access with an institutional account.

Documenso is also designed to integrate with external services. In our implementation we take advantage of three of them. For document storage, instead of keeping the PDFs on the server disk, we delegate them to MinIO — an object server compatible with Amazon's S3 protocol — running on FACET's own infrastructure. For email notifications, we use Brevo as an SMTP relay, with its free tier comfortably covering our sending volume. And for authentication, we enabled OAuth 2.0 with Google, which means lecturers sign in directly with their UNT Google account, without creating or remembering another password. [PAUSE]

FACET's infrastructure already had Proxmox VE — an open-source hypervisor. Proxmox supports two types of virtualization: full virtual machines with KVM, and lightweight containers with LXC. We used an LXC container because of its low overhead. [CLICK] Inside that container we installed Coolify, an open-source PaaS platform that manages the entire service lifecycle. Coolify includes Traefik as an integrated reverse proxy: it handles TLS certificates and automatic HTTP routing. Each system component — Documenso, MinIO, the database — runs in its own fully isolated Docker container. [PAUSE]

Once the service architecture was resolved, the next step was to size the object storage in MinIO. We did not assign resources 'by eye': the calculation starts from the real operational limit of the email provider. Brevo allows 300 emails per day. An average procedure requires 6 notifications, which gives a ceiling of 50 documents per day. Each procedure generates 2 PDFs of 10 MB on average, that is 20 MB per procedure, which gives 1 GB of daily growth. I configured a 5-year ILM retention policy for regulatory reasons — 1,825 days times 1 GB gives us a projected 1.825 TB. That is why I assigned a strict 2 TB quota, with a 10% margin. And as you can see in the chart, those 2 TB represent barely 11% of the TrueNAS server's 18+ TiB at the faculty.

Backup and Retention Policies

Database
PostgreSQL
  • Frequency: daily — 3:00 AM
  • Content: PostgreSQL .dmp file
  • Retention: 30 days
Configuration
Docker Compose + .env
  • Frequency: daily — 4:00 AM
  • Content: compose + .env + certificate .p12
  • Retention: 180 days
Operating On-Premise means the faculty assumes full responsibility for continuity. I designed the strategy in two domains. First, the database: PostgreSQL runs an automatic logical dump every day at 3 in the morning, into an isolated MinIO bucket. Retention is 30 days with Object Lock in governance mode — that is, nobody can delete or modify those dumps during that period. Maximum RPO of 24 hours, and restoration is done directly from the Coolify interface. Second, the configuration: a bash script runs every day at 4 in the morning — one hour after the dump — and packages the docker-compose files, the environment variables and, fundamentally, the Base64-encoded .p12 signing certificate. That certificate is what gives each generated signature its institutional identity. Without it, even with the database recovered, I cannot issue new signatures. With this backup, I rebuild the entire environment in minutes.

At rest, the four containers consume only ~500 MB of RAM.

To confirm that the allocated hardware was sufficient, I subjected the system to a burst of five documents sent for signing at the same time, monitored with Netdata. At rest, the whole system consumes barely 400 MB of RAM. During the burst, we observed this stepped behavior: that is Browserless spawning processes to render the PDFs, reaching a peak of 1.33 GB of RAM and using 12.5% of processing. The result is conclusive: we have more than 83% free memory and 87% free CPU. The system is extremely lightweight and the faculty has enormous room for growth.
